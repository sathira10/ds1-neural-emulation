\chapter{Literature Review}

\section{Purpose and Structure of the Review}

This chapter establishes the technical and musical context for modelling the Boss DS-1 as a nonlinear audio system. It moves from established virtual analog approaches toward data-driven neural modelling, identifies why recurrent architectures are appropriate for distortion circuits with memory-dependent behaviour, and builds the research gap for a direct GRU versus LSTM comparison under matched recording, training, and deployment conditions.

\section{Analog Distortion as a Musical and Technical Phenomenon}

The pursuit of digitally emulating analog audio hardware, a discipline known as virtual analog (VA) modelling, is driven by a persistent demand for the sonic character of classic equipment. Terms such as ``warmth'', ``richness'', ``character'', and ``organic feel'' are imprecise, but they refer to audible results of nonlinear electronic components operating near or beyond their intended linear regions. When an audio signal passes through a vacuum tube, transistor, or diode in this region, the device introduces new frequency content that was not present in the original signal. This phenomenon is known as \textbf{harmonic distortion}, and it is central to the sound commonly associated with analog equipment.

This sound, now a cornerstone of modern guitar music, was not originally a deliberate invention. It emerged from the limitations and failures of early amplification technology. Its origins can be traced to the 1950s, when musicians discovered that overdriving amplifiers produced an unintentionally powerful fuzzy sound. The 1951 recording of \textit{Rocket 88} is often cited in this history, as a damaged amplifier speaker cone produced a novel distorted tone that became part of the record's identity. After this, musicians began actively seeking this raw sound, including by deliberately pushing or damaging their equipment.

The commercialization of this effect in the 1960s was marked by the Gibson Maestro FZ-1 Fuzz-Tone, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:maestro-fz1}. Its later association with The Rolling Stones helped demonstrate that distortion could be treated as a controllable musical effect rather than merely a technical fault. This encouraged manufacturers to develop a wide range of distortion devices that shaped the sound of later rock, punk, metal, and alternative music.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{images/maestro_fz1.jpg}
    \caption{Gibson Maestro FZ-1 Fuzz-Tone pedal.}
    \label{fig:maestro-fz1}
\end{figure}

The specific character of a distortion effect is determined by the blend of harmonics it generates, which is dictated by the circuit topology. Two broad categories are useful here:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Even-order harmonics} are integer multiples such as two, four, and six times the fundamental frequency. They are generally perceived as musically consonant and can add smoothness and constructive brightness. The asymmetrical clipping introduced by a single-ended transistor stage, for example, can produce even-order harmonics.
    \item \textbf{Odd-order harmonics} are integer multiples such as three, five, and seven times the fundamental frequency. They tend to sound harsher or grittier, contributing to the richness and depth of many distortion sounds. The symmetric clipping of back-to-back diodes is a common design that mainly generates odd-order harmonics.
\end{itemize}

Beyond simple harmonic generation, the perceived warmth of analog equipment arises from subtle imperfections such as transformer hysteresis, dynamic compression, and circuit sag under heavy load. It is this complex and dynamic interplay of nonlinearities that gives analog distortion its sonic identity, and it has proven difficult to capture perfectly in the digital domain.

\section{The Boss DS-1 as a Modelling Target}

To grasp the challenge of emulation, it is useful to analyse a specific and widely recognised circuit. The Boss DS-1, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:boss-ds1-pedal}, was released in 1978 and is one of the best-selling distortion pedals in history. Its architecture represents a classic approach to creating an aggressive, high-gain guitar tone.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.55\textwidth]{images/boss_ds1_pedal.jpg}
    \caption{Boss DS-1 distortion pedal.}
    \label{fig:boss-ds1-pedal}
\end{figure}

The DS-1 circuit, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:boss-ds1-circuit}, can be understood as a sequence of distinct audio stages:

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{images/boss_ds1_circuit.jpg}
    \caption{Boss DS-1 circuit used as the modelling target.}
    \label{fig:boss-ds1-circuit}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Input buffer stage}: The circuit begins with a transistor, typically a 2SC2240, in an emitter-follower configuration. This stage provides high input impedance, which is important when interfacing with passive guitar pickups. It preserves the instrument's high-frequency content and tone, ensuring that the pedal does not negatively affect the sound when bypassed.

    \item \textbf{Transistor booster stage}: Following the buffer is a common-emitter amplifier stage. This stage provides a fixed amount of voltage gain, approximately 35~dB, and incorporates high-pass filtering to remove excessive low-frequency content before the main distortion stage. This pre-clipping bass cut prevents the distortion from becoming muddy and tightens the sound. The stage can also be driven into soft, asymmetrical clipping, adding even-order harmonic content before the primary distortion occurs.

    \item \textbf{Op-amp gain and hard-clipping stage}: This is the heart of the effect. A non-inverting operational amplifier provides variable gain controlled by the user's Distortion knob. The defining feature of the DS-1 is its \textbf{hard-clipping} topology. The amplified signal is fed to a pair of anti-parallel silicon diodes connected between the signal path and a reference voltage. When the voltage exceeds the forward voltage of the diodes, approximately \(\pm 0.7\)~V, the diodes conduct and provide a path to the reference voltage. This sharply truncates the waveform rather than gently rounding it, producing strong odd-order harmonics that are perceived as aggressive, bright, and harmonically complex.

    \item \textbf{Tone and level stage}: After clipping, the DS-1's sound is further shaped by a passive tone control circuit. This tone stack is similar in principle to the Electro-Harmonix Big Muff Pi tone control. It consists of a fixed low-pass filter and a fixed high-pass filter, with the Tone knob acting as a blend between their outputs. The component values create a significant mid-range scoop centred around approximately 500~Hz. By reducing mid-range content and emphasizing bass and treble, the DS-1 produces a larger sound in isolation, a sonic signature common in hard rock and metal contexts.
\end{enumerate}

This multi-stage design, with its combination of transistor and op-amp gain, hard-clipping diodes, and distinctive tonal shaping, presents a nuanced target for digital emulation.

\section{Virtual Analog Modelling Approaches}

\subsection{White-Box Circuit Modelling}

Historically, virtual analog modelling has been approached through two broad paradigms: white-box and black-box modelling. Each represents a different philosophy and involves trade-offs between accuracy, interpretability, and computational feasibility.

White-box, or physical, modelling is based on the idea that a sufficiently complete mathematical description of an analog circuit can be solved digitally to reproduce its output for a given input. The process begins with a detailed circuit schematic. Using principles such as Kirchhoff's current and voltage laws, the circuit is translated into a system of nonlinear \textbf{ordinary differential equations (ODEs)}. Software such as \textbf{SPICE} automates this process using techniques such as Modified Nodal Analysis (MNA) to construct and solve the system of equations. Other white-box techniques include Wave Digital Filters (WDFs) and state-space models, which provide different formalisms for representing circuit behaviour.

The primary advantage of white-box modelling is its potential accuracy and interpretability. In principle, an accurate circuit model can explain the contribution of each component and reproduce the device under a wide range of operating conditions. However, this approach has two important limitations for real-time audio processing.

First, the computational cost can be prohibitive. Distortion circuits contain nonlinear elements, so their systems of equations generally cannot be solved directly at each sample. The simulator must use an iterative numerical method, such as Newton--Raphson iteration, to converge on a solution. Performing this process for every audio sample is expensive, and the sequential nature of the computation limits parallelization. A detailed SPICE simulation may therefore run much slower than real time.

Second, white-box modelling requires detailed and accurate knowledge of the circuit and its components. The accuracy of the simulation is limited by the quality of the component models and parameter values supplied to it. Real analog components vary across manufacturing tolerances and age, so a model based only on ideal datasheet values may not capture the behaviour of a specific physical unit. To perfectly model an imperfect analog device, one would first need to characterize those imperfections, which is often impractical.

\subsection{Traditional Black-Box and Signal Processing Methods}

Black-box modelling approaches provide an alternative to circuit-level simulation by treating the target device as an input-output system. Instead of deriving a full set of component equations, the modeller records how the device responds to known signals and then designs a digital system that reproduces the same behaviour. In the case of a guitar distortion pedal, this means using a dry input signal as the model input and the corresponding pedal output as the target. This approach is attractive because it avoids some of the practical difficulties of white-box modelling, such as measuring exact component values, modelling non-ideal semiconductor behaviour, and simulating nonlinear differential equations at audio rate \cite{martinez_ramirez_deep_2020,wright_real-time_2019}.

The simplest black-box approach to distortion is static waveshaping. A waveshaper applies a nonlinear transfer function to each input sample independently. For example, a soft-clipping curve may round off high input amplitudes, while a hard-clipping curve may flatten the waveform above a threshold. This is useful because it captures the basic fact that distortion is an amplitude-dependent process: louder samples are changed more strongly than quieter samples. Many musical distortion sounds can be approximated by placing filtering before and after such a nonlinearity. Yeh et al. describe this kind of filter--distortion--filter structure as a computationally efficient simplification of analog distortion pedals, where conditioning filters shape the signal before clipping and equalisation filters shape the result afterwards \cite{yeh_simplified_2007}. This is closely related to the practical design of many guitar effects, where the audible character of the pedal is produced not only by the clipping element but also by the frequency-dependent stages around it.

However, a memoryless waveshaper is limited because it assumes that the output at time (n) depends only on the input at time (n). This is not fully true for real analog pedals. The Boss DS-1 contains capacitors, filters, transistor and op-amp stages, a diode clipping section, and a tone network. These stages do not simply map each sample to a new sample independently; they also respond to recent signal history. For example, high-pass and band-pass filtering alter the phase and envelope of the signal over time, while reactive components store and release energy. The resulting output therefore depends on the present input and on the recent past. A static nonlinearity can reproduce a broad clipping shape, but it cannot fully represent the way filtering, clipping, and dynamic level-dependent behaviour interact across time.

Linear convolution is another useful black-box technique. If a system is linear and time-invariant, its behaviour can be captured by an impulse response. This makes convolution highly effective for modelling reverbs, cabinets, microphones, EQ curves, and other systems where the output is a scaled and filtered version of the input. In guitar processing, convolution is widely used for loudspeaker cabinet simulation because a cabinet and microphone chain can often be approximated as a linear filter over a useful operating range. The limitation is that a nonlinear distortion pedal cannot be described by a single impulse response. When the input level changes, the harmonic content generated by the device changes too. A quiet signal may pass with relatively little alteration, while a loud transient may produce strong clipping, compression, and harmonic expansion. A fixed impulse response cannot generate new harmonics that were not already present in the input.

More advanced traditional black-box methods attempt to combine linear filtering and nonlinear mapping. Wiener, Hammerstein, and Wiener--Hammerstein models are common examples. A Wiener model places a linear filter before a static nonlinearity, while a Hammerstein model places the nonlinearity before the filter. A Wiener--Hammerstein model combines both by placing a nonlinearity between two linear stages. These structures are more expressive than plain waveshaping because they can represent a simplified chain of filtering and nonlinear processing. Damskägg et al. note that such block-oriented models have been used in virtual analog modelling, but they still require assumptions about the structure of the device \cite{wright_real-time_2019}. This can be useful when the modeller knows the circuit topology, but it becomes restrictive if the device contains several interacting nonlinear and reactive stages.

For the DS-1, this distinction matters. The pedal is not just a clipping curve. Its transistor booster, op-amp clipping section, and tone stage all contribute to the final sound, and the order of these stages is musically significant. The pre-clipping filtering helps determine what frequencies drive the diodes hardest, while the post-clipping tone section reshapes the newly generated harmonic content. A traditional block model may capture the broad sonic identity of the pedal, but matching the detailed input-output behaviour of one physical unit can still be difficult. This motivates the move toward neural black-box modelling, where the model structure is not required to explicitly mirror the circuit, but is trained to approximate the measured behaviour directly.

\section{Deep Learning for Nonlinear Audio Systems}

Deep learning extends black-box modelling by replacing a hand-designed model structure with a trainable function. The model is shown many examples of input audio and target output audio, and its parameters are adjusted so that its output becomes closer to the recorded device output. In this project, the conceptual setup is straightforward: the dry guitar recording is the input and the Boss DS-1 processed recording is the target. The model is not told where the diodes, capacitors, or filters are in the circuit. Instead, it learns a mapping from dry waveform to distorted waveform from paired recordings.

This is particularly suitable when the research goal is not to explain every internal circuit state, but to create a model that behaves closely enough for audio use. Martínez Ramírez et al. describe deep learning for audio effects as an end-to-end black-box strategy in which raw audio is used as both input and output, reducing the need for detailed prior knowledge of the processor being modelled \cite{martinez_ramirez_deep_2020}. The advantage is flexibility: the same broad modelling framework can be applied to distortion pedals, amplifiers, compressors, equalisation, and time-varying effects, provided that suitable training data can be captured. The disadvantage is reduced interpretability. A trained neural network may produce an accurate output, but its internal weights do not provide the same kind of circuit explanation as a schematic or state-space model.

Several strands of recent work support the use of deep neural networks for virtual analog audio. Damskägg et al. modelled several distortion pedals, including the Boss DS-1, using a feedforward WaveNet-style architecture and showed that such models can run in real time on a consumer desktop computer \cite{wright_real-time_2019}. Their work is especially relevant because it treats the DS-1 as one of the target devices and frames neural modelling as a practical route for nonlinear distortion circuits. WaveNet-style models are based on stacks of causal convolutional layers. Each layer applies filtering and nonlinear activation, and dilated convolutions allow the network to observe a wider time window without requiring an impractically large number of layers. In this sense, the receptive field of the model acts as a learned memory window.

However, the choice of architecture affects the trade-off between accuracy, complexity, and real-time suitability. A large convolutional model may be accurate, but it can be computationally expensive. Wright et al. therefore proposed compact recurrent neural networks for real-time black-box modelling of amplifiers and distortion pedals, comparing LSTM and GRU units against WaveNet-style models \cite{wright_real-time_2019}. Their results are important because they show that recurrent models can achieve comparable accuracy with lower processing cost. This is directly relevant to plugin deployment, where a model must run inside the audio callback with predictable timing.

Practical open-source projects have also influenced this area. Tools such as GuitarLSTM and SmartGuitarAmp demonstrate that neural amplifier and pedal models can be trained in Python and deployed in JUCE-based plugins \cite{GuitarML_GuitarLSTM,GuitarML_SmartGuitarAmp}. These projects are not a substitute for peer-reviewed evaluation, but they are relevant to a practice-based dissertation because they show a working path from dataset capture to trained model to musician-facing software. This project follows a similar practical chain: record the target pedal, train compact recurrent models, export the weights, load them in a C++ plugin, and evaluate whether the resulting artefact is accurate and efficient enough for real-time use.

\section{Sequence Modelling and Recurrent Neural Networks for Audio Emulation}

Audio is a time sequence. Each sample occurs in context, and the perceived sound depends on how samples evolve over time. This is especially important for nonlinear audio systems with memory. A memoryless model can only respond to the instantaneous sample value, but an analog circuit with capacitors and filters responds to recent signal history. For example, a high-pass stage does not merely change the amplitude of one sample; it changes the relationship between present and past samples. Similarly, clipping after filtering will depend on the signal level and spectral balance arriving at the clipping stage over time.

Recurrent neural networks are designed for this kind of sequential problem. A recurrent model processes one time step at a time while maintaining an internal state. In audio terms, the model can be viewed as receiving the current input sample and a summary of previous samples, then producing the next output sample. Wright et al. express this general idea as a model whose predicted output depends on the current input, the previous recurrent state, and the learned parameters \cite{wright_real-time_2019}. This makes RNNs conceptually well matched to circuits where the present output depends on both current excitation and recent history.

The hidden state is the key idea. It is not audio in the ordinary sense, and it is not a direct measurement of a capacitor voltage or transistor current. Instead, it is a learned internal representation that carries useful information forward through time. During training, the model learns what information to store in this state because storing the right information reduces prediction error. For a distortion pedal, the hidden state may help represent recent level, envelope, spectral balance, or other context that affects the next output sample. This allows a compact model to learn dynamic behaviour without explicitly simulating each component in the circuit.

RNNs are also attractive for real-time plugins because they can be causal. A causal model only uses the present and past; it does not require future samples. This is essential for low-latency audio processing, since a plugin cannot look ahead without adding delay. A recurrent model can process samples one by one as they arrive from the host. The hidden state must then be preserved across processing blocks, because resetting the state at every buffer boundary would create discontinuities and would prevent the model from carrying musical context across time. This is a practical but important point: the architecture is not only a training choice, but also a deployment choice.

There are challenges. RNNs can be harder to train than feedforward networks because gradients must be propagated through time. Long audio files contain very large numbers of samples, so training typically uses shorter sequence segments and truncated back-propagation through time. The aim is to give the model enough context to learn useful memory effects while keeping training feasible. These details are more methodological than musical, but they matter because they determine whether a model can learn stable behaviour rather than only matching short fragments.

\section{LSTM and GRU Architectures}

Basic recurrent networks can suffer from unstable training and difficulty preserving information over longer sequences. LSTM and GRU architectures address this by using gates. A gate is a learned control mechanism that decides how much information should be kept, updated, or discarded. In audio modelling, this is useful because not all past information is equally relevant. A recent transient, a decaying resonance, and a stable low-level input may each require different forms of memory.

\subsection{Long Short-Term Memory}

The Long Short-Term Memory architecture was introduced to improve the ability of recurrent networks to preserve information over time \cite{hochreiter_long_1997}. An LSTM contains a hidden state and a cell state. The cell state acts as a longer-term information path, while the gates regulate how new information enters, how old information is retained, and how much of the stored information is exposed to the output. In simplified terms, the LSTM can choose when to remember, when to forget, and when to use its memory.

For nonlinear audio emulation, this extra structure can be valuable. Distortion circuits may involve short-term filtering, envelope-dependent response, and level-dependent nonlinear behaviour. A model with stronger memory control may therefore track the target more accurately, particularly when the input includes changes in dynamics, palm-muted transients, sustained notes, or rapid note attacks. Wright et al. found that LSTM models performed strongly in their real-time black-box modelling experiments, and in their reported comparison the LSTM generally outperformed the GRU overall \cite{wright_real-time_2019}. Cassidy and De Sena also used an LSTM-RNN as one of the main model types in their perceptual evaluation of high-gain amplifier modelling, showing that recurrent models remain relevant beyond purely objective waveform matching \cite{cassidy_perceptual_2023}.

The main drawback is cost. An LSTM has more gate groups than a GRU, so for the same hidden size it usually has more trainable parameters and requires more computation per sample. In an offline rendering context this may be acceptable, but a real-time plugin has stricter constraints. Every sample must be processed quickly and predictably. A model that is slightly more accurate but significantly heavier may be less attractive if it limits the number of plugin instances, increases CPU load, or risks audio dropouts at small buffer sizes.

\subsection{Gated Recurrent Unit}

The Gated Recurrent Unit is a simpler gated recurrent architecture \cite{cho_learning_2014}. It combines aspects of memory and hidden state into a more compact structure and uses fewer gates than an LSTM. The practical consequence is that a GRU with the same hidden size usually has fewer parameters and lower computational cost. This makes the GRU appealing for real-time audio, where efficiency is not secondary but part of the model's suitability.

The potential trade-off is representational capacity. Because the GRU has a simpler structure, it may not preserve and regulate temporal information as flexibly as an LSTM in every task. However, this does not mean that it is automatically worse for a distortion pedal. The best architecture depends on the target system, the amount and type of training data, the chosen hidden size, and the deployment constraints. A DS-1 at one fixed setting may not require the same long-term memory capacity as a time-varying modulation effect or a compressor with long release times. If the GRU achieves similar objective error with fewer parameters and lower CPU cost, it may be the stronger practical choice for a plugin, even if the LSTM is slightly more expressive in principle.

This is why a direct GRU versus LSTM comparison is useful. The question is not whether LSTM or GRU is universally better, but which architecture provides the best accuracy-to-efficiency balance for this captured DS-1 modelling task. The comparison should therefore consider objective error, parameter count, exported model size, inference cost, and observed behaviour in the plugin. This framing avoids treating neural modelling as a purely academic prediction problem and instead evaluates it as an audio engineering problem.

\section{Real-Time Deployment and Neural Inference}

A trained model is only useful for this dissertation if it can be deployed in a real-time audio context. Training in Python is convenient because frameworks such as PyTorch provide automatic differentiation, GPU support, and flexible experimentation. A DAW plugin, however, cannot normally depend on a Python training environment. It must load a trained model and perform only forward inference, usually in C++.

Real-time audio imposes strict constraints. The audio thread must avoid operations that can take an unpredictable amount of time, such as memory allocation, file access, locks, or other blocking operations. Chowdhury presents RTNeural as a lightweight C++ neural inference library designed for hard real-time systems, with support for relevant layer types including dense, Conv1D, LSTM, and GRU layers \cite{chowdhury_rtneural_2021}. This makes it directly relevant to the current project, where the trained recurrent models are exported from the training pipeline and loaded in a JUCE/RTNeural plugin for sample-by-sample inference.

The deployment context also affects architectural judgement. A model that is accurate in Python but too slow or inconvenient to load in a plugin does not fully answer the research question. Conversely, a compact model that performs reliably in the plugin may be preferable even if a larger model produces a marginally lower test error. This is especially true for guitar effects, where low latency and stable performance are part of the playing experience. A guitarist does not experience the model as an error metric; they experience it as a responsive effect in a signal chain.

\section{Evaluation of Neural Audio Models}

Evaluation of neural audio models usually begins with objective error metrics. In this project, Error-to-Signal Ratio (ESR) is the main objective measure. ESR compares the energy of the prediction error to the energy of the target signal. In plain form, it can be expressed as: ESR = sum of squared target-minus-model error divided by sum of squared target signal. This avoids judging the model only by absolute error, because the error is interpreted relative to the level of the recorded pedal output.

ESR is useful because it gives a repeatable way to compare the GRU and LSTM under the same test conditions. It is common in neural virtual analog work, including the RNN experiments by Wright et al. and the perceptual amplifier study by Cassidy and De Sena \cite{wright_real-time_2019,cassidy_perceptual_2023}. Some training pipelines also use pre-emphasis filtering so that the loss gives more attention to high-frequency content, which is important for distortion because clipping generates upper harmonics that strongly affect brightness and edge \cite{wright_real-time_2019}.

A DC error term is also useful. A neural model may learn a small constant offset that is not musically intended. Even if this offset is not immediately obvious as distortion tone, it can reduce headroom, interact badly with later processing, or indicate low-frequency bias in the model. Wright et al. added a DC term to their loss because early model outputs showed DC offset \cite{wright_real-time_2019}. Including DC error as a supplementary measure therefore supports a more stable audio engineering evaluation.

Objective waveform metrics still have limits. A lower ESR does not automatically mean that listeners will prefer the model, or even that they will always hear it as closer. Small waveform differences can be perceptually irrelevant, while some spectral or dynamic differences may be clearly audible even if the overall error appears modest. Cassidy and De Sena address this issue by evaluating high-gain amplifier models using preference, realism, perceptual accuracy, ABX, and MUSHRA-like tests \cite{cassidy_perceptual_2023}. Their work shows that neural models can be judged differently depending on whether the question is accuracy, realism, or preference.

For this reason, the evaluation in this project should be framed carefully. Objective metrics can answer whether one model better matches the recorded DS-1 waveform under the test conditions. Parameter counts and plugin observations can answer whether one model is more efficient or practical for deployment. Formal listening tests, such as ABX or MUSHRA-style evaluation, would be needed to make stronger perceptual claims \cite{ITU_BS1534_3,cassidy_perceptual_2023}. Since this project is scoped around objective comparison and plugin implementation, perceptual equivalence should remain a future-work claim rather than a completed result.

\section{Research Gap and Positioning of This Study}

The reviewed literature shows that virtual analog modelling of distortion can be approached in several ways. White-box and physically informed methods provide interpretability and can reproduce important circuit behaviour, but they require detailed circuit knowledge and may become computationally demanding. Traditional black-box structures such as waveshaping, convolution, and Wiener/Hammerstein models can be efficient, but they often rely on simplifying assumptions about the order and type of linear and nonlinear stages. Deep neural networks offer a more flexible data-driven alternative by learning the input-output mapping directly from paired recordings \cite{yeh_simplified_2007,martinez_ramirez_deep_2020}.

Within neural virtual analog work, several important precedents already exist. Damskägg et al. demonstrated real-time WaveNet-style modelling of distortion pedals including the Boss DS-1 \cite{wright_real-time_2019}. Wright et al. showed that compact recurrent networks can model nonlinear audio devices with substantially lower processing cost than some WaveNet configurations, and that LSTM and GRU architectures can be compared within the same broad real-time black-box framework \cite{wright_real-time_2019}. Martínez Ramírez et al. placed these developments in a wider deep-learning audio-effects context, showing that architectures with explicit temporal modelling are important for systems with longer dependencies \cite{martinez_ramirez_deep_2020}. Cassidy and De Sena further show that objective accuracy should be interpreted alongside perceptual questions, especially for high-gain guitar sounds \cite{cassidy_perceptual_2023}.

The specific gap addressed by this dissertation is narrower and practice-based. Prior work has shown that neural networks can model distortion pedals, and that recurrent models can be efficient for real-time audio. However, there remains value in a controlled GRU versus LSTM comparison on a single physical Boss DS-1 pedal, using the same recording chain, the same fixed knob setting, the same train/validation/test split, the same training pipeline, and the same plugin deployment path. This matched comparison is important because architecture comparisons can be misleading if the dataset, target device, hyperparameters, or deployment conditions differ.

This project therefore contributes not by proposing a new neural architecture, but by documenting and evaluating a complete end-to-end workflow for one well-defined modelling target. The artefacts include the matched dry/wet DS-1 dataset, trained LSTM and GRU models, exported JSON weights, and a JUCE/RTNeural plugin capable of loading and running the models. This aligns with practice-based research because the knowledge is produced through building, testing, and reflecting on the artefact as well as through reviewing prior literature \cite{candy_practice-based_2018,batty_research_2024}. The research gap is therefore both technical and practical: determining which compact recurrent architecture offers the stronger balance of accuracy, efficiency, and deployability for this specific DS-1 emulation task.
