\chapter{Results}
\label{chap:results}

\section{Chapter Overview}
\label{sec:results-overview}

This chapter reports the empirical results of the project, covering the final dataset, training behaviour, objective test-set evaluation, exported model artefacts, and plugin runtime observations. The aim is to present the measured outcomes of the workflow clearly and directly, leaving their broader interpretation to Chapter~5.

The chapter draws on the completed LSTM and GRU training runs, the exported JSON model files, and the final plugin tests carried out in digital audio workstation environments. Error-to-Signal Ratio (ESR) is treated as the primary objective accuracy metric, supported by SNR, correlation, RMSE, and MAE. Informal listening observations are included only as practical notes from deployment and are not presented as a substitute for formal perceptual evaluation.

\section{Dataset and Experimental Artefacts}
\label{sec:dataset-and-experimental-artefacts}

The final dataset consisted of matched dry input and Boss DS-1 target recordings divided into training, validation, and test splits. All audio files used for training and evaluation were recorded and processed at 44.1 kHz. The dataset was organised as paired mono input and target WAV files, allowing the recurrent models to learn a direct sample-level mapping from the dry guitar signal to the corresponding processed DS-1 output.

The DS-1 pedal setting and recording signal chain are documented in Appendix A. The present chapter reports the measured dataset properties and model outcomes rather than repeating the full recording methodology.

\section{Training Behaviour}
\label{sec:training-behaviour}

Both recurrent models were trained using the same training pipeline so that the LSTM and GRU could be compared under matched conditions. The training procedure used the Adam optimiser, an initial learning rate of 0.005, weight decay of \(1 \times 10^{-4}\), and a validation-based learning-rate scheduler. The loss function combined pre-emphasised ESR and DC loss using the weighting \(0.75 \times \mathrm{ESRPre} + 0.25 \times \mathrm{DC}\).

Figure~\ref{fig:loss_curves} shows the training and validation loss curves for both models. The LSTM continued improving for a longer period and reached its best validation result at epoch 284. The GRU reached its best validation result earlier, at epoch 86, and stopped after a shorter training run. This indicates that the GRU converged earlier under the current configuration, while the LSTM ultimately achieved the lower validation loss.

\section{Objective Test-Set Evaluation}
\label{sec:objective-test-set-evaluation}

The held-out test set was used for objective comparison between each model output and the recorded Boss DS-1 target signal. The best-validation checkpoint is treated as the primary model for comparison, because this checkpoint represents the model state selected by validation performance rather than by the final training epoch.

ESR was used as the main objective accuracy metric because it measures model error energy relative to the target signal energy. SNR and correlation were included as supporting metrics. In this context, SNR should be understood as an error-relative signal-to-noise ratio, where the “noise” is the prediction error rather than physical noise in the recording chain. Correlation provides a broad measure of waveform similarity, but it is not treated as a perceptual quality measure.

The LSTM achieved the lowest test-set ESR, with a best-validation checkpoint ESR of 0.021411. The GRU achieved a higher best-validation checkpoint ESR of 0.035938. The same ordering is shown by the supporting metrics: the LSTM produced lower RMSE and MAE, higher error-relative SNR, and higher waveform correlation than the GRU on the held-out test material.

The comparison between best-validation and final checkpoints also supports the use of validation-selected checkpoints for reporting. The LSTM final checkpoint was very close to its best-validation checkpoint, with only a small increase in ESR. The GRU showed a larger difference between the best-validation and final checkpoint results, indicating that the final model state was less favourable than the checkpoint selected by validation performance.

\section{Architecture and Parameter Comparison}
\label{sec:architecture-and-parameter-comparison}

Both models used the same compact plugin-compatible recurrent architecture. Each model was implemented as a mono \texttt{SimpleRNN} with one recurrent layer, hidden size 24, a dense output stage, and a skip connection. The only architectural change in the comparison was the recurrent unit type: one model used an LSTM cell and the other used a GRU cell. The hidden size of 24 was retained for the final comparison so that both architectures could be evaluated under the same deployment constraint. The implications of this model-size constraint are discussed further in Chapter~5.

\section{Plugin Runtime Observations}
\label{sec:plugin-runtime-observations}

The exported models were tested in the deployed JUCE and RTNeural plugin. The plugin was built and tested as VST3 and AU. Runtime testing was carried out in Ableton Live 12 and Cubase 12 on an M2 Max MacBook Pro with 32 GB unified memory running the current macOS Tahoe installation used for the project.

The plugin exposed the main controls required for the research artefact: Drive, Level, Bypass, and Model selection. Both LSTM and GRU models loaded correctly and could be selected from within the plugin. No model-loading errors, control-related artefacts, or audible artefacts during knob movement were observed during the final tests.

Model switching between LSTM and GRU also behaved correctly in the tested hosts. The hidden state was reset during switching, and no switching artefacts were observed during the reported tests. This supports the plugin’s use as a practical comparison artefact for the two exported recurrent models.

No formal CPU benchmark was completed. Therefore, this chapter does not report a measured CPU percentage or computational load figure. The plugin did not introduce additional algorithmic latency, because inference was performed sample-by-sample inside the host audio callback and no explicit look-ahead or additional buffering stage was added. This statement should not be read as a measured end-to-end latency benchmark.

\section{Summary of Results}
\label{sec:summary-of-results}

The results show a clear objective difference between the two recurrent architectures under the matched hidden-size-24 configuration. The LSTM achieved the lower held-out test ESR, lower RMSE and MAE, higher error-relative SNR, and higher waveform correlation. The GRU used fewer parameters and stopped much earlier during training, but its validation and test-set error remained higher than the LSTM.

The exported JSON artefacts were successfully loaded into the JUCE and RTNeural plugin, and the plugin operated correctly in the tested VST3 and AU host environments. Drive, Level, Bypass, and Model switching behaved as expected, and no switching or knob-movement artefacts were observed during the final tests. However, no formal CPU benchmark was completed, and the non-44.1 kHz resampling path was not yet implemented and verified.

Overall, the measured results indicate that the LSTM provided the stronger objective emulation accuracy, while the GRU provided the smaller model. Chapter 5 interprets this accuracy-efficiency trade-off in relation to the research question, the hidden-size-24 constraint, and the practical requirements of real-time neural audio plugin deployment.
